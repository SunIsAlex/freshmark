#PartI:Alcohol substitution

ROH + HX

Conclusion:

  1. Mechanism: hydroxyl oxonium ion (water is a good leaving group)
  2. Small sterically hindered primary alcohol, otherwise SN1S_{N1} will rearrange
  3. Alcohol reactivity: allyl type, benzyl type, 3°>2°>1°
  4. Hydrohalic acid reactivity: HI>HBr>HCl\ce{HI\gt HBr\gt HCl}

Neighbor participation effect

In order to explain the racemic product and the faster reaction rate, in connection with the three pairs of lone pairs of electrons of the Br atom, the neighbor group participation effect was proposed.

Neighbor group participation requires stereochemistry: trans coplanar

The lone pair of electrons of the bromine atom fills the antibonding orbital of the carbon-oxygen bond, causing the departure of water to form Bronium ion.

Next, the bromium ion is attacked by BrX−\ce{Br-} to open the ring, and attacks from the left and right are possible, so two racemates with a chirality ratio of 50:50 are generated.

ROH + PBr3

3 ROH+PXX3→3 RX+HX3POX3ROH+PXX5→RX+POXX3+HX\begin{gathered} \ce{3ROH + PX3 -> 3RX + H3PO3}\\ \ce{ROH + PX5 -> RX + POX3 + HX} \end{gathered}P is a third period element, so the P atom in PBrX3\ce{PBr3} has a 3d orbital and can be attacked by the oxygen lone pair of electrons of the hydroxyl group, turning the hydroxyl group into a good leaving group and producing the nucleophile BrX−\ce{Br-}.

Correction (added by Claude) Explaining this step with "P has 3d orbitals" is an older picture. Computational chemistry shows that third-period 3d orbitals are too high in energy to take meaningful part in bonding. What actually happens: the alcohol oxygen attacks P as a nucleophile and displaces one BrX−\ce{Br-}(P stays three-coordinate, so no expanded octet is needed), giving RO−PBrX2HX+\ce{RO-PBr2H+}. The BrX−\ce{Br-} then attacks the carbon from the back side (SN2S_N2), and HOPBrX2\ce{HOPBr2} leaves. This is also why bromination of a chiral secondary alcohol with PBrX3\ce{PBr3} inverts its configuration.

Also, "Hoffmann" under regioselectivity below should be Hofmann (Hofmann's rule).

Conclusion:

  1. Chlorination: PClX3/PClX5\ce{PCl3}/\ce{PCl5} Bromination: PBrX3\ce{PBr3} (phosphorus pentabromide is unstable) Iodination: P+IX2\ce{P + I2}
  2. Mainly used for 1°, 2°
  3. Low temperature to avoid rearrangement

ROH + SOCl2

ROH+SOClX2→△SOX2↑+HCl↑+RCl\ce{ROH + SOCl2 ->[\triangle] SO2 ^ + HCl ^ + RCl}

Reaction advantages: directly obtain alkyl chloride, mild conditions, fast rate, high yield, and the product is easy to purify.

The configuration of the alcohol is maintained before and after the reaction, and chlorosulfite can be separated at low temperature and decomposed into alkyl chloride and SOX2\ce{SO2} upon heating, which indicates that the reaction mechanism is:

During the reaction, chlorosulfite is first generated, and then decomposes into a tight ion pair. ClX−\ce{Cl-} attacks the carbocation as part of the leaving group (LG), that is, "returns internally" to obtain the product chloroalkane with maintained configuration. Since the substitution seems to be carried out within the molecule, it is called intramolecular nucleophilic substitution, represented by SNiS_Ni.

If the weak nucleophile pyridine is added to a mixture of alcohol and thionyl chloride, a product with a configuration flip is obtained.

The generated ClX−\ce{Cl-} is used as a nucleophile to attack chlorosulfite from the back side of the carbon-oxygen bond to obtain a configuration-inverted product (SN2S_N2)

The reaction of tertiary amines (R3NR_3N) and HCl is also conducive to the formation of chloride ions, so tertiary amines, like pyridine, can catalyze this reaction: R3N+HCl→R3NH++Cl−R_3N + HCl \rightarrow R_3NH^+ + Cl^-
